Atom for teachers

Atom is a free, open-source periodic table that computes every element from the equations and lets students build, predict, and work through the physics of any atom. There are no accounts, no servers, and nothing is sent anywhere. It runs in any modern browser, works offline after the first visit (Chromebook carts and iPads included), and every lab can be assigned with one link.

Assign anything with a link

Paste a link into your LMS, a slide, or a worksheet. It opens the lab on the element and step you chose. Students need nothing but a browser.

Inside The math, the Copy link button makes the link for whatever is on the screen, and Worksheet prints the ten questions for that element with the answer key on its own last page. In Predict, a challenge is a short code (such as A1.k3j9.10.nqcsb.H-He-Li-C) that gives everyone the same questions in the same order; the Make a challenge panel builds one from the elements and question types you check, "Common mistakes" included.

Six twenty-minute activities

1. Read the shells from a spectrum

Electron energies (PES) · AP Chemistry 1.5 and 1.6 · NGSS HS-PS1-1

Open sodium. Students count the electrons in each peak from the left, write the configuration, then press Mystery element and identify five elements from their spectra alone. Collect: the five configurations, written out.

Say plainly that the peak positions are estimates (Slater's rules); the pattern is the point. Then open Trends and ask where the dashed estimate leaves the measured line, and why (p electrons).

2. What is the Sun made of?

The Sun · NGSS HS-PS4-3, HS-ESS1-3

Open The Sun. Students try each element and record which ones leave dark lines. Ask why helium gives no dark line even though it is the Sun's second ingredient (the surface is too cool for helium to absorb; it was found in 1868 as a bright line in an eclipse, 27 years before it was found on Earth). Collect: the list of five, and one sentence on how we know what a star is made of without going there.

3. The empty atom

The math, step 1 and step 9 · Predict, "Common mistakes" · NGSS HS-PS1-1

Open iron at step 9 and have students read the nucleus radius, then step 1 for the atom's. Ask for the ratio before showing it. Then the iron challenge: six "common mistakes" questions, one of which asks the same thing with a 1-meter nucleus. Collect: a turn-in code (below).

4. Why the orbit cannot last

The math, steps 2 and 3 · NGSS HS-PS4-3 · AP Chemistry 1.5

Open hydrogen at step 2. Students read the orbit's speed and period, then step 3: an accelerating charge radiates, and the atom would collapse in about 16 picoseconds. Ask what a theory has to add to stop that (step 4: Bohr's rule), and what Bohr could not explain (step 5). Collect: the worksheet for hydrogen, questions 2 to 4.

5. Mass that disappears

The math, step 9 · Nuclides · NGSS HS-PS1-8

Open iron-56 at step 9: the five pieces of its binding energy, and the 0.5 u that is missing when the nucleus forms. Then hydrogen, where the formula fails and says so. Finish on the chart of the nuclides: which way does iron's neighborhood decay? Collect: question 9 from each worksheet.

6. Which electrons leave first?

Build, Predict · AP Chemistry 1.5 and 1.8 · NGSS HS-PS1-1

Open Build on iron and remove two electrons; the configuration readout shows which ones went. The iron challenge asks it directly (question 4 of six). Collect: a turn-in code.

A first lesson, minute by minute

Forty-five minutes on iron, for a class that has never opened Atom. Everything students need is one link; the lesson is also built in, under Lessons, as "Atoms are mostly empty", with the same steps and an exit check at the end.

  • 0 to 5Open the table on the projector. Click iron. Scroll once: the cloud, then the light, then the empty space. Ask: if the nucleus were a marble on your desk, how far away would the electrons be? Take guesses and write three on the board.
  • 5 to 15Students open Build on iron. Add and remove protons, neutrons, and electrons and read what changes: the element, the isotope, the charge. Ask each pair to make a stable isotope, a radioactive one, and an ion, and to write the three names down.
  • 15 to 25Open The math at step 9. Read the nucleus radius out loud, then step 1 for the atom's. Have students compute the ratio before Atom shows it, then scale the nucleus to a marble and check the board's guesses. A one-centimeter marble puts the electrons about 300 meters away, three football fields; the exit check asks the same with a one-meter nucleus, and the answer is 30 kilometers.
  • 25 to 35Open Predict on iron and let students work through a few questions. Wrong answers come back on their own ten minutes later, so a student who misses one gets a second try without being told to.
  • 35 to 42The exit check: the iron challenge, six "common mistakes" questions in a fixed order, the same for everyone. Students press Copy my turn-in code under You and paste it into your form or LMS.
  • 42 to 45Ask the class for the one thing that surprised them. The usual answers are the emptiness, that the electron has no path, and that the nucleus weighs less than its parts. All three are where the next lessons go.

If devices are short, the first fifteen minutes work on a projector alone; Build and Predict want one screen per pair.

Five lesson paths with exit checks

The Lessons tab holds five paths of about 45 minutes each. Every step opens the lab it needs and is ticked when a student finishes it; the last step is an exit check, a fixed set of six questions, the same for the whole class, whose score is written into the student's turn-in code. Languages and reading support

Atom is in English and Spanish, with more languages on the way. The language menu is in the top bar of every page, and a link can carry the choice: ?lang=es opens the site in Spanish, ?lang=es#lab=build&z=26 opens a lab in Spanish. Element names, every lab, every question, and The math are translated; the measured data and the references stay as they are.

Under Learning settings in the labs, three helps can be turned on for a student or a class, and each one stays on in that browser:

  • Simpler rewrites the explanations in shorter sentences and plainer words, with the same facts and the same numbers. Good for younger readers and for students reading in their second language.
  • Term help underlines the science words. Tapping one shows a one-line meaning; in Spanish, it shows the English word too, so a bilingual class builds vocabulary in both languages at once. Vocabulary prints the words on the current page in both languages for a word wall or a glossary sheet.
  • Listen buttons read the explanations aloud with the device's own voice, in the page's language. Sound is off until a button is pressed.

Found a translation that reads wrong? Every string lives in app/i18n/es.js on GitHub, and a corrected line is a one-line pull request.

Piloting Atom in your class

If you try Atom with a class, a short report helps the next teacher. The one-page handout prints what students and families need to know. After a lesson, the classroom report form on GitHub asks six questions: grade and subject, class size and devices, which labs or lesson you used, what worked, what did not, and anything that confused students. Nothing about students goes in it; it is about the software. Reports are read, and they shape what gets built next.

Collecting work without accounts

Atom keeps progress in the browser only. For a class, use one of these:

  • Turn-in codes. In You, a student presses Copy my turn-in code and pastes it into your form or LMS. The code is one line with the date, the counts (explored, built, mastered, right answers, streak), which elements, and which lessons were finished with their exit-check scores, with a check digit and no name. Paste a class's codes into Read the codes on the same tab for a table.
  • Worksheets. The math's Worksheet prints ten questions for the chosen element; the answer key is the last page, so print all but the last for students.
  • Print my progress. One page with everything a student has done, from the You tab.
  • Shared computers. Save my progress downloads (and copies) a small file; Bring progress back merges it in on the next machine.

What is computed and what is measured

Atom tells students which is which, and the labs are built to show the seams. Electron clouds are computed from the Schrödinger equation for one electron with Slater's effective charge; the measured first ionization energies are from NIST, and Trends draws Slater's estimate against them so the gap is visible. The binding energies in step 9 use the liquid-drop formula where no measurement exists and the AME2020 measured value where one does, and the step says so. Spectral lines are NIST measurements; hydrogen's are computed from the Rydberg formula. Every constant is CODATA 2022, and the full source list is at the bottom of the main page.

Privacy

Atom makes no network requests after it loads and keeps no accounts. Progress, the theme, and the picture-quality choice live in the student's own browser. A link or a turn-in code carries only what it shows. The code is open: github.com/Normansrule/atom, MIT License.
